Origin

A hundred dollars of lawn-mowing money

I was twelve, the bike was a Yamaha YZ100F, and I bought it from a kid across town without telling my parents. There was a spark plug wire involved, and a ten-dollar cable, and three weeks I did not need to spend.

Brandon Meredith · Origin · 8 minute read

I bought my first motorcycle at twelve years old. A Yamaha YZ100F, a hundred dollars, from a kid across town who had outgrown it. I paid in cash I had made cutting grass all summer, in bills that had been folded and counted and folded again so many times they had gone soft as cloth.

My parents did not know. My parents would not have approved. I had considered this at some length and concluded that I did not care.

The waiting was the longest part

Before there was a bike there were the magazines. Dirt Bike and Dirt Rider, every issue, bought with the same lawn money and read until the staples let go. I knew machines I would never sit on the way other kids knew batting averages. I could have told you what a works pipe did for a two-stroke powerband. I could not have told you what a fuel shutoff valve did, which turns out to be the load-bearing detail of this story.

That went on for years. Not months — years, in a state of patient, total wanting. School happened somewhere in the background. The bike was the foreground, and it did not exist yet.

So when a friend mentioned that somebody across town had a YZ100F he wanted a hundred dollars for, it was not really a decision. It was more like a door opening in a wall I had been staring at for a very long time.

“Here, hold this”

Before any money changed hands, the kid took me out to the trails to show me what I would be getting. This was the eighties, and we had room to roam — nothing official, nothing posted, just a lot of dirt that nobody had gotten around to caring about yet.

The bike would not start. He kicked it a dozen times, swore at it with real fluency for someone that age, and then stopped, pulled the spark plug wire off the plug, and held it out to me.

“Here, hold this.”

I held it. He kicked.

I have since learned that the magneto on a small two-stroke puts out something in the tens of thousands of volts at almost no current, which is the entire reason this qualifies as a diagnostic procedure rather than an assault. At twelve I had no framework for any of that. What I had was a jolt that started in my hand, went up through both shoulders, and left somewhere around my back teeth.

He took the wire back and nodded, satisfied.

“Okay. Magneto’s fine.”

It was a fouled plug. He knew that before he handed me the wire, I am fairly sure. He knew a startling amount about that motorcycle, in the specific way that kids who have to fix their own things know things — no theory, no manual, just a working map of every part of it that had ever failed. What he did not appear to have, ever, was a dollar to spend on it. The bike showed it.

The clutch, for example, did not work. Not “felt a little grabby.” Did not work. Changing gear meant matching engine speed to road speed by ear and feel and moving the lever at exactly the right moment, and finding neutral was less a gear selection than a negotiation. Getting under way at all involved a procedure I would not dignify by calling it a technique.

I rode it around those trails for twenty minutes. I wanted it more than I have wanted anything since.

The handover

It is hard to explain what it feels like at twelve to hand over money you earned yourself for a thing you have wanted that badly. Excited does not cover it. I was excited and I was also quietly terrified, which I now recognize as the correct response to finally getting what you want.

The kid took my soft handful of bills and went inside.

I had a few miles to get home and a motorcycle to do it on. A motorcycle with no clutch, which I knew about, and one other problem, which I did not.

It ran beautifully for about a quarter mile

Then it started to stumble. Then it surged, stumbled again, caught, and went quiet, and I was standing at the side of the road holding up a motorcycle that no longer worked, with no idea why.

Here is what had happened, though it would be a while before I understood it. The previous owner had turned the fuel off at the petcock — which is exactly what you do when a bike is going to sit, and the single most responsible thing he did in the entire transaction. That left whatever was already in the float bowl. The float bowl held about a quarter mile.

Fifteen seconds and one small lever would have fixed it. Nobody had told me that lever existed. It had never once appeared in a magazine.

The push

So I pushed it home.

A YZ100F is not a heavy motorcycle. It is an extremely heavy thing-a-twelve-year-old-pushes-up-a-hill. The route out had been pleasantly downhill, which should have told me something about the route back and did not.

I was a scrawny kid at that age. Athletic, but assembled mostly out of coat hangers. That push must have left me sore for days. I have no memory whatsoever of being sore, because I had a motorcycle in my garage, and there is no channel anywhere in a twelve-year-old’s nervous system reserved for reporting that kind of information.

I remember being furious for about the first mile. I remember the fury burning off somewhere in the second and leaving behind something flatter and more stubborn. I remember not once considering leaving the bike where it stood, not seriously, not for a second — it had cost me an entire summer of somebody else’s lawns.

And I remember thinking about those magazines, and noticing for the first time that in all those hundreds of photographs, nobody was ever pushing.

The best day of my life turned into a long afternoon of work. I got it home anyway.

Then I took the whole thing apart

The clutch was the first order of business. It was the thing I knew was broken, and it was now my broken thing, which made it the best problem I had ever had.

I had no idea how a clutch worked. I had no manual, no tools worth the name, and no adult I could ask without disclosing that I owned a motorcycle. So I did the only thing available to me: I started removing parts until I found it.

Half that bike spent the better part of a week on the front lawn. Fasteners went into coffee cans in an organizational scheme I would describe as optimistic. I was filthy and completely, idiotically happy.

Eventually I got inside the engine case, and there it was — the clutch pack, and the push lever that works it. I moved that lever back and forth with my thumb and watched the plates pull apart and squeeze together, apart and together, doing the exact thing that the lever on the handlebar was supposed to make them do. I had never seen the inside of a machine do the thing the machine does. I am not sure I can convey what that did to me. I suspect I have been chasing it ever since.

The plates looked fine. The basket looked fine. The lever moved exactly the way it was supposed to move.

Which left me precisely where I had started, minus one motorcycle, which was now in pieces on the grass.

So I spent ten dollars on a new clutch cable, on the sophisticated engineering theory that it was the only part of the system I had not yet stared at. It came in the mail. I put the bike back together. I hooked up the cable, kicked it over, and pulled the lever.

The clutch worked perfectly.

I want to be accurate about the next two seconds, because they arrived in a specific order. The first was pure, uncut triumph — the best feeling I had ever had, a thing I fixed, working, because of me.

The second was: wait.

Wait. Was the clutch always fine? Did I just disassemble an entire motorcycle in my front yard, over the course of three weeks, because of a ten-dollar cable that the previous owner had simply never gotten around to buying?

Yes. That is exactly what happened.

The RC car

I did eventually find out what he spent my hundred dollars on.

A radio-controlled car. Disassembled. A box of parts.

He had traded a running motorcycle — a real one, two wheels, an engine, noise — for a toy car that did not run and, from what I could see of it, never had. I remember standing there looking into that shoebox of plastic and thinking, with the full moral authority of a seventh grader, what is wrong with you.

I have softened on him since. Some. The truth is we were both twelve, and being twelve is a condition in which you can hold a motorcycle in one hand and a spectacularly bad idea in the other and be genuinely unable to tell which is which. I had just spent three weeks in my own front yard demonstrating the same principle from the opposite direction.

He got a box of parts. I got the long straw, and I have never stopped being grateful for it.

Motorcycles have never once given me what I expected in the way I expected it. I have never stopped wanting them.

Why I’m telling you this

That summer did not put me off motorcycles even slightly. It has never once occurred to me that it should have.

Two things did come out of it that I have never really put down.

The first is that the thing standing between you and the ride is almost never the hard thing. It is the small thing nobody mentioned. A petcock. A ten-dollar cable. Between those two items I lost three weeks and several miles of uphill pushing, and neither one was difficult — they were just invisible to me, and nobody had any reason to point at them.

What stops you is rarely the hard thing. It’s the small thing nobody mentioned.

The second is that other people’s carelessness becomes yours the moment you take delivery. Nobody hands over a machine with a list of what they let slide. You inherit all of it at once, and you find out one item at a time, usually at the side of a road.

Which is, more or less, what a review is for. Not the horsepower figure — you can get that anywhere. The list of small invisible things that will be standing between you and the ride, eighteen months from now, when the shine is off and you are a long way from a dealer.

Bikes break at inconvenient times. They are hot and loud and expensive and the weather is usually not on your side. The wanting does not go anywhere. If anything the wanting seems to be made partly out of the trouble.

Years later I would learn that a kick-start bike mostly does not catch on the first kick, that this is normal, and that it is the second kick that tells you what sort of day you are about to have. I didn’t know that yet either. At twelve I only knew that I had a motorcycle — eventually, after the pushing, and the disassembly, and the cable.

I am starting over with a few things at the moment, and I notice I am going at it roughly the way I went at that hill. Head down. Not especially graceful. Unwilling to leave the thing in the road.

This channel is named for the kick that catches. But I have come around to thinking the pushing is the part that actually counts.
