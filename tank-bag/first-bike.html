A hundred dollars of lawn-mowing money

I was twelve, the bike was a Yamaha YZ100F, and I bought it from a kid across town without telling my parents. There was a spark plug wire involved, and a ten-dollar cable, and three weeks I did not need to spend.

Brandon Meredith · Origin · 6 minute read

I bought my first motorcycle at twelve years old. A Yamaha YZ100F, a hundred dollars, from a kid across town who had somehow grown bored of it. I paid in cash I had made cutting grass all summer, in bills that had been folded and counted and folded again so many times they had gone soft as cloth.

My parents did not know. My mother would definitely not have approved. I had considered this at some length and concluded that I did not care. I would probably be grounded for a decade or longer, but I would be a grounded kid with a motorcycle and that was a big improvement from where I was.

You see, before there was a bike, there were the magazines. Dirt Bike and Dirt Rider, every issue, bought with the same lawn money and read until the staples let go. I knew machines I would never sit on the way other kids knew batting averages. Yet little details such as fuel shutoff switches received very little attention in such magazines. More on that soon.

My obsession dragged on for years, much to my mother's chagrin. Her obsession with safety and the absolute exclusion of all things fun in my life was nearly equal to my own obsession with dirt bikes.

Finally a school friend offered to take me with him to the local trails. This was the eighties, and we had room to roam — nothing official, nothing posted, just a forest full of well ridden trails and unicorns, or so I assumed. I remember every detail of that day, including the far from safe and far from legal ride across town, the fillup with leaded fuel on pocket change, and the careful addition of oil from a slim necked plastic bottle he kept in his coat pocket.

“Here, hold this”

We get to the trailhead and the bike won't start. Kick after kick, my dreams were being crushed. I prayed while he swore at it with impressive fluency. Finally he stopped, pulled the spark plug wire and held it out to me.

“Here, hold this.”

I held it. He kicked. I have since learned that the magneto on a small two-stroke puts out something in the tens of thousands of volts. An effective but sadistic diagnostic procedure. I felt it from my palm to my teeth and everywhere in between.

He took the wire back and nodded, satisfied.

“Okay. Magneto’s fine.”

It was a fouled plug. A quick rub with sandpaper from the tool pouch, and we were in business. I rode on the back as we bounced around the trails and had the time of our lives. At least I did. The bike needed some special techniques, it had no torque and no working clutch, so we had to get it moving before kicking into gear, then I would race to hop on before it stalled. It was amazing.

Not long after, he said he was thinking of selling the bike, and I jumped at the chance. I couldn't believe a kid my age would GET RID of a bike, but his loss was my gain. We negotiated $100, everything I had.

The handover

The kid took my soft handful of bills and said he'd see me at school Monday. I had a few miles to get home, but now a motorcycle to do it on. Already familiar with the clutch technique, I was off.

It ran beautifully for about a quarter mile

Then it started to stumble. Then it surged, stumbled again, caught, and went quiet, and I was standing at the side of the road holding up a motorcycle that no longer worked, with no idea why.

He had shut off the petcock, probably the responsible thing to do, but that possibility never crossed my mind because the bike was running just fine up until then, obviously fuel wasn't the problem. Plus there was plenty still left in the tank. I know now that the float bowl holds about a quarter mile.

The push

So I pushed it home.

A YZ100F is not a heavy motorcycle on its own. It is an extremely heavy thing-a-twelve-year-old-pushes-up-a-hill. I was a scrawny kid, athletic, but assembled out of coat hangers. That push must have left me sore for days. I have no memory whatsoever of being sore, because I had a motorcycle in my garage.

The best day of my life turned into a long afternoon of work. I got it home anyway.

Then I took the whole thing apart

I'll skip over the part where I was in big trouble, and my mother cried, and my father tried to reason with her ("He already paid the kid honey..."). Ultimately my mother typed out a long list of "motorcycle rules" which curiously included "NO RACING SQUIRRELS", and life moved on.

The clutch was the first order of business. It was the thing I knew was broken, and it was now my broken thing, which made it the best problem I had ever had.

I had no idea how a clutch worked. I had no manual, no tools of my own, and no adult I could ask without disclosing that I owned a motorcycle. So I did the only thing available to me: I started removing parts until I found it.

Half that bike spent the better part of a week on the front lawn. Fasteners went into coffee cans in an organizational scheme I would describe as optimistic. I was filthy and idiotically happy.

Eventually I got inside the engine case, and there it was — the clutch pack, and the push lever that works it. I moved that lever back and forth with my thumb and watched the plates pull apart and squeeze together, apart and together, doing the exact thing that the lever on the handlebar was supposed to make them do.

The plates looked fine to my untrained eye. The basket looked fine. The lever moved exactly the way it was supposed to move.

Which left me precisely where I had started, minus one motorcycle, which was now in pieces on the grass.

So I spent ten dollars on a new clutch cable from the local bike shop, hoping to get to the bottom of why this sophisticated piece of engineering was failing me, and obviously the boy I bought it from. This must be a very tricky problem if he never solved it himself. But I had determination on my side.

I put the bike back together, hooked up the cable, kicked it over, and pulled the lever. The clutch worked perfectly.

I want to be accurate about the next two seconds, because they arrived in a specific order. The first was pure, uncut triumph — a thing I fixed on a real motorcycle, working, because of me.

The second was: wait.

Wait. Was the clutch always fine? Did I just disassemble an entire motorcycle over the course of three weeks because of a ten-dollar cable that my friend had simply never gotten around to buying?

Yes. That is exactly what happened.

The RC car

I did eventually find out what he spent my hundred dollars on.

A radio-controlled car. Disassembled. A box of parts. When I saw it in his bedroom I couldn't believe my eyes.

He had traded a running motorcycle — a real one, two wheels, an engine — for a toy car that did not run and, from what I could tell, never had. I remember standing there looking into that shoebox of plastic and springs thinking, with the full moral authority of a seventh grader, what is wrong with you.

Being twelve is a condition in which you can hold a motorcycle in one hand and a spectacularly bad idea in the other, and be genuinely unable to tell which is which. I had just spent three weeks in my own front yard demonstrating the same principle from the opposite direction.

He got a box of parts. I got the long straw, and I have never stopped being grateful for it.

Why I’m telling you this

I am starting over with a few things at the moment, and I am going at it roughly the way I went at that hill. Head down and pushing as hard as I can, unwilling to leave the thing in the road.

This channel is named for the kick that catches. But I have come around to thinking the pushing is the part that actually counts.
