From the Tank Bag

Same 720°, different rhythm

Every four-stroke engine fires each cylinder once for every two turns of the crankshaft: 720 degrees. How an engine spaces those bangs around the clock changes how it sounds, how it shakes, and how well you can feel the rear tire. Pick three engines, press play, and watch. Better yet, turn the sound on.

Playback
Sound and view

Keyboard: Space plays and pauses, R restarts, F toggles full screen. Sound plays one engine at a time; press the sound button to move from the first to the third.

How to read it

Each dial is one full cycle, so the hand makes one lap for every two turns of the crank. The numbered markers are the cylinders, in the order they fire, and each one flashes when it goes. The yellow arc is the longest quiet stretch between one bang and the next.

On a wide screen, the strip underneath lays the same rhythm out in a line across four turns of the crank, so you can compare engines beat for beat.

Things to try

  • Put the 90° V-twin next to the 270° parallel twin. The dials are identical, which is why a Ténéré 700 sounds a little like a Ducati.
  • Set the speed to Brisk, turn the sound on, and listen to the single, then the 270° twin, then the crossplane four.
  • Compare the 360° twin with the boxer. Same rhythm, very different motorcycles. Whatever makes a boxer special, it isn't the beat.

Why the gaps matter

You'd think the smoothest, most even rhythm would always win. On the highway, it often does. But in the dirt, the engines riders trust most tend to leave space between bangs: the single, the 270° twin that powers most of today's middleweight adventure bikes, even Triumph's T-plane triple, which gave up a perfectly even beat on the Tiger 900.

Nobody fully agrees on why. One idea is that the tire gets a moment to grab between hits. Cycle World's Kevin Cameron argued it's less about grip than feel: an uneven engine lets the rider sense the tire starting to slide, and ride closer to the limit because of it. Yamaha's argument is about noise, the constant surge and sag in crank speed caused by heavy pistons stopping and starting. Take that out, and what reaches the tire is what your right hand asked for.
